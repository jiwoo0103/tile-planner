## What an allowance adds

A tile waste allowance increases the quantity estimated from floor area. It is a planning margin for material that may not become part of the finished surface, plus any spare stock you choose to keep. It does not mean that the stated percentage of your order will definitely be thrown away.

Start with measured dimensions and the exact product's pack information. If those are uncertain, use the [room measuring guide](/guides/how-to-measure-a-room-for-tile/) first. Increasing an allowance cannot fix a missing floor section or an incorrect tile size.

## Separate cuts, breakage and future repairs

These serve different purposes when you discuss an order with your installer:

- **Cutting allowance** covers material lost when tiles are shaped for edges, corners or openings. A leftover piece helps only if its dimensions, orientation and usable edges fit another position. Counting every cut piece as a whole discarded tile can overstate losses; assuming every offcut will be reusable can understate them.
- **Breakage allowance** covers accidental damage during handling or installation. This is uncertainty in completing today's work, rather than a planned feature of the finished layout.
- **Repair stock** is usable tile deliberately retained after the installation. Decide on that reserve separately so that material consumed by cuts or breakage is not also promised for future repairs.

Fireclay Tile's [ordering FAQ](https://www.fireclaytile.com/a/faqs) identifies cuts, accidental damage and future repairs as reasons for ordering extra. It also warns that a later add-on order may not match the original batch exactly. Those points explain why spare material can have value beyond the installation day; they do not predict how many tiles your job will consume.

The calculator has **one combined allowance field**. It does not allocate that amount among these purposes or guarantee a number of intact tiles remaining at the end. If an installer has already included an allowance in a quantity, establish what it covers before adding another margin.

## Conditions to review before choosing a percentage

Use the layout and product together when reviewing the allowance. Two floors with the same area can require different cuts.

- **Room outline and openings:** compare a simple rectangle with recesses, columns and several doorways. Mark the locations that need shaped pieces on your sketch.
- **Pattern and direction:** check the actual repeat and edge cuts. Diagonal or herringbone work needs its own review; this site's straight-grid preview does not model those patterns.
- **Tile size and edge requirements:** consider whether offcuts fit elsewhere and whether a particular edge or face direction must be retained. A count of visible cut pieces alone does not answer this.
- **Handling and cutting plan:** discuss the selected material, intended tools and trial cuts with the installer. An allowance should reflect the job being planned, rather than a fixed guess based only on floor area.
- **Replacement availability:** ask whether the chosen product can be reordered and how batch variation affects replacements. Decide what usable stock you want to store after completion.

For a concrete example of **manufacturer guidance**, Fireclay's FAQ recommends at least 15% overage for its standard patterns and 20% for complex patterns such as herringbone. Its [quantity article](https://www.fireclaytile.com/blogs/fireclay-tile-blog/tile-school-doing-the-math) discusses 15–20% and a minimum of 20% for herringbone in the context of its handmade products. These are Fireclay's recommendations for its products and ordering context, not universal rates for every tile. Confirm the current guidance for the actual product and installation with your supplier or installer.

“Required tiles” includes the chosen allowance. “Box extras” means purchased tiles minus required tiles: it counts the surplus introduced by rounding up to whole boxes, **not** all material above the area-only estimate. No cost is shown because the price is blank.

For the 10% row, 120 × 1.10 = 132 required tiles. Rounding 132 ÷ 10 up gives 14 boxes and 140 purchased tiles. The allowance adds 12 tiles to the area estimate; box rounding adds another 8. Together they make 20 tiles above the base count. None of these numbers is a promise of 20 intact spare tiles after installation.

The 15% row also buys 14 boxes: 120 × 1.15 = 138, and 138 ÷ 10 still rounds up to 14. Raising the allowance has used more of the same purchase quantity as a planning margin, leaving only 2 box extras. At 20%, 144 required tiles cross the next box boundary, so the estimate becomes 15 boxes. Purchases change in steps, even though the percentage changes smoothly.

## Apply the percentage before rounding

The calculator first divides room area by tile area. It multiplies that **unrounded area ratio** by 1 + allowance ÷ 100, then rounds up to a whole required tile. It rounds up again to whole boxes. The separately displayed base tile count is already rounded up and should not be used as the starting quantity for applying the percentage.

This example's area ratio happens to be a whole number. For your own measurements, keep the full precision until the rounding steps in the [calculator's calculation method](/calculator/#calculation-method). Do not increase the room dimensions to simulate allowance and then also enter the same allowance percentage; that would apply extra material twice.

## Turn the estimate into an order discussion

Bring your measurements, product details and proposed pattern to the supplier or installer. Agree on what the allowance covers and how much usable repair stock should remain. Check the actual box quantity and compare the resulting purchase with the supplier's stated coverage. Trim and other separately sold pieces need their own quantity review.

Use the [2D layout preview](/layout/) to inspect straight rectangular grids and edge-piece dimensions. Its full-tile and cut-piece counts describe placements, not a cutting plan or a minimum order. Neither that preview nor the area calculator works out how to reuse offcuts, subtracts grout area from purchasing, or predicts breakage.

Once the assumptions are settled, [open the tile calculator](/calculator/) with your own measurements and agreed allowance. Review the total tiles purchased as well as the required tiles, and keep the intended repair reserve in mind when checking the final order.

For a complete example with a fractional base ratio, full-box rounding and a hypothetical budget, follow the [tile box purchase example](/guides/tile-box-purchase-example/).
